I was going to study plants.

Three Dianthus. Three emotional climates. One carefully designed experiment about whether human intention shapes biological growth.

Process Note — Why this experiment?

I came into this project genuinely curious, not performing curiosity. I had been reading The Secret Life of Plants and kept getting stuck on one idea: Cleve Backster's plant responded to his intention to harm it before he did anything physical. The polygraph spiked the moment the thought formed. Not when he acted. When he decided.

That felt important. If intention is a real variable — if living systems pick up on what you're about to do — then the emotional climate around a plant might matter as much as the water and light schedule.

So I designed an experiment that isolated that variable. Three Dianthus plants, identical in every physical condition. The only difference: what I brought into the room with me. Positive attention for one. Negative discharge for another. Indifference for the third.

The data I was going to collect: plant height weekly, leaf count, bud count, flower count, a visual vitality rating. Alongside that, a daily emotional log. Two parallel datasets. Biological and emotional, running in sync. I thought the interesting finding would be in the plants.

The plants died by week seven.

But the experiment didn't.

Process Note — What actually happened

The first week proceeded largely as designed. I sat near the affirmation plant and spoke to it, which felt strange and then less strange. The neutral plant was avoided deliberately, which felt stranger still. I didn't engage with the discharge plant — there was not enough negative emotion available on demand. That observation, minor at the time, proved significant in retrospect.

By week two, the structure had collapsed under academic deadlines and the diffuse semester fog that dissolves daily routine. Watering became sporadic. Logging ceased. By week seven the leaves were curling and the plants were beyond recovery.

What became clear only in retrospect: the experiment had not entirely failed. It had simply measured something unanticipated. The experiment was measuring me before it ever measured the plants — an experiment designed to test whether emotional presence affects living systems had revealed that consistent emotional presence could not be sustained, and that inability constituted its own category of finding.

While attempting to sustain emotional presence toward three potted plants, I observed that a far more complex version of the same experiment was already running.

On the self. Unconsciously. Continuously. Without controlled conditions.

The body had been storing emotional data about places, sounds, and situations for years, retrieving that data automatically and shaping behaviour accordingly — faster than conscious thought could intervene.

The Backster connection: his plant responded before the physical event. My body responded before my conscious mind processed the context. The mechanism is different — I am not claiming I am a plant — but the structure is identical. A living system that has stored information about a previous experience responds to the signal that experience is nearby. Automatically. Without asking permission.

The research question shifted: what emotional data has the nervous system stored, and can awareness of that storage alter its effect on present-moment experience?

Process Note — The somatic memory framework

Somatic memory refers to physiological and emotional information stored in the nervous system as a result of lived experience. Unlike declarative memory, somatic memory operates below the threshold of conscious thought — retrieved faster than cognitive processing, efficient as a survival mechanism, but difficult to examine through deliberate reflection alone.

The complication arises when a stored pattern no longer reflects current conditions: when a response appropriate to an earlier context continues to fire in response to superficial similarity, regardless of whether the original conditions remain present. The work was to identify such patterns, bring them into conscious awareness, and observe whether recognition altered the intensity of the automatic response.

The Emotional Geography

Six locations. Each carrying a distinct somatic charge. The body's response is real. Its source, in most cases, is historical.

Hostel room — lights after midnight
Autopilot 7/10 — anxiety→After recognition 4/10

Lights left on past midnight produce a reliable response: low dread, a feeling of being trapped in wakefulness. Originated with a previous roommate who never turned off lights — months of disrupted sleep. The current conditions are different. The body does not know that.

Staircase window
Autopilot 6/10 — dread→After recognition 3/10

A palimpsest memory: two distinct emotional associations occupying the same spatial coordinates. A job interview overlaid anticipatory dread onto a previously positive association. Recognising the two layers as separate made the original calm available again.

Lift area window
Autopilot 7/10 — trapped/bored→After recognition 2/10

Felt, with startling intensity, trapped — bored in a way that felt almost existential. The response arrived before conscious processing. Rewrote the memory in real time: chose to look at the view as something available rather than evidence of confinement. The fastest and most complete shift in the study.

Campus roads at night
Autopilot 6/10 — alertness→After recognition 5/10

Repeated monkey encounters have extended the threat response beyond actual sightings: anyone carrying food outdoors now produces a spike of alertness. Textbook conditioned fear generalisation. Harder to revise because the original association was grounded in real repeated experience.

Sister's house
Autopilot 6/10 — survival-anxiety→After recognition 4/10

During a period when she was still learning independent living, her home was associated with scarcity: missed meals, minimal supplies. She is now a capable and generous host. The house is different. The background anxiety that precedes arrival has not fully updated.

Parents' house and home state
Autopilot 8/10 — old feelings→After recognition 6/10

The highest density and longest history of any location in the study. Familiar surroundings through the bus window and the old feelings begin returning — before arriving, before speaking to anyone. A seventeen-year-old's feelings, running in a twenty-two-year-old body. The most resistant location.

Process Note — Data collection method

Triggers were recorded as encountered, not sought out. Each entry captured: the trigger, the quality and intensity of the automatic response (1–10), the probable historical origin, and a second rating taken after conscious recognition was applied.

The supporting practices — meditation, mindfulness, deliberate stillness — were not data collection instruments. They were the conditions that made noticing possible.

The Timeline

The data I actually collected this semester wasn't biological. It was temporal. A record of the experiment as it actually happened — and as it pivoted.

Early semester

Plant experiment begins. Three Dianthus purchased. Week one proceeds as designed — sitting with the affirmation plant, avoiding the neutral one, finding there isn't enough negative emotion on demand for the discharge condition.

Weeks 2–3

Structure collapses under academic deadlines and semester fog. Watering becomes sporadic. Logging ceases. The designed experiment fails quietly — and measures something unintended in doing so.

Week 7

Plants die. Leaves curling, beyond recovery. The formal biological experiment cannot be recovered. Recognition arrives: "The experiment was measuring me before it ever measured the plants."

Mid semester

Methodological pivot. The subject becomes the self. Daily meditation and mindfulness established. Social media and passive scrolling discontinued. Data collection begins — spontaneous, encounter-triggered, honest.

Late semester

Intentional auditory conditioning underway. Teahouse becomes a completed conditioning case — 8/10 reliable calm. Hymn for the Scarecrow approximately 60% established. The original experiment's logic, directed inward.

End of semester

Six spatial locations and five songs documented. Central finding: conscious recognition reliably reduces intensity but rarely eliminates it. Depth of conditioning determines resistance to revision. The experiment is still running.

Process Note — How I reconstructed this

This timeline is reconstructed, not recorded in real time. The exact dates are approximate; the sequence is what I'm confident about. I worked backwards from what I know clearly — the week the plants died, the midterm submission date, the period when the practices became consistent.

The lesson — that methodology requires consistent documentation to produce reliable temporal data — is one I understand now in my body, not just as a principle.

The Data

Two categories of trigger documented across eleven weeks: spatial (places) and auditory (songs). Both produced automatic physiological responses. Both were measurable before and after conscious recognition was applied.

How the scale works

A 10 means the response shapes behaviour — I avoid the place, or enter it carrying the charge. A 1 means there is barely anything there, or what is there is so integrated it no longer interrupts.

The second number is never zero. Recognition does not erase the response. It creates space around it. That space is what changes the experience of being in the place.

♪ The auditory data

Sound proved the most efficient trigger. The body's response to specific songs was faster and more total than any spatial trigger — possibly because music bypasses some of the cognitive processing that spatial navigation involves, arriving directly at the emotional and physiological systems.

Banana Fish OST produced 9/10 grief regardless of current emotional state — a somatic memory operating entirely independently of present mood. The Adults Are Talking by The Strokes, once associated with significant lostness, has naturally remapped to 2/10 as life circumstances changed. Evidence that somatic associations are not permanent.

Teahouse is a completed intentional conditioning case: 8/10 reliable calm, built through deliberate association with states of ease. This is the direct descendant of the original plant experiment's logic — structured emotional intention, applied consistently, directed inward.

Process Note — Limitations of this data

All data is self-reported by one person. There is no external validation, no control group, and no way to verify whether the reported changes in response intensity reflect genuine physiological change or shifts in conscious framing. The 1–10 scale measures perceived intensity, not physiological response.

What this method provides is phenomenological data — what it was like from inside. That is a legitimate but different category of evidence. These limitations do not invalidate the findings. They define their scope.

The Secret Life
of Plants

In Backster's framework,
I was the plant.

The book's most extensively discussed case is Cleve Backster, who connected a galvanic skin response monitor to a plant in 1966 and observed that the plant registered a response at the moment he formed the intention to harm it — before any physical action was taken. The response preceded the event. Backster termed this "primary perception": living systems respond to intention rather than to physical stimulus alone.

The broader methodological finding — one that undermined the book's scientific credibility while proving philosophically generative for this project — was that experimental results could not be reliably separated from the emotional state of the researcher. Calm and genuinely curious experimenters produced consistent plant responses; anxious or performatively sceptical researchers produced noise. The observer could not be controlled for.

I went into my experiment wanting to study plants. I was the thing that couldn't be controlled for. My inconsistency, my forgetting, my inability to sustain presence — that was the variable that wrecked the original design and revealed the more interesting question.

Running responses to stimuli I hadn't consciously registered. Needing someone to ask: what are you actually reacting to here?

Process Note — The experimenter problem

There is no clean separation between the observer and the observed. The experiment is always, partially, about the experimenter. In this project, that problem is not a methodological flaw to be managed. It is the design. I am the researcher and the subject and the instrument simultaneously.

The places and relationships carrying the most complex emotional memory are, with few exceptions, those with the longest and deepest shared history. Familiarity accumulates emotional data — not always coherent. The same place may be associated with joy and grief and guilt and warmth, stored in overlapping layers. Awareness-based work here is slower and more effortful because there is more data to examine, more competing patterns to distinguish, and more history in which the present moment might become confused with the past.

What I Learned

Recognition reduces.
It does not erase.

Recognition reduces. It does not erase. In every case, awareness produced a measurable reduction in the intensity of a conditioned response. In no case did it eliminate the response entirely. What changes is not the pattern but the relationship to it.

Depth of history determines resistance to revision. The oldest associations — the parental home, years of accumulated grief in a song — were the most resistant. The shallowest and most recently formed shifted most readily. Difficulty of revision is proportional to duration and intensity of the original experience.

The body responds before the mind catches up. In every documented case, the somatic response arrived before the researcher had consciously evaluated the current situation. The intervention point is not before the response. It is in the moment immediately after it arrives.

Intentional conditioning works in both directions. Existing associations can be partially revised. New positive associations can be deliberately built. Teahouse is a completed case. Hymn for the Scarecrow is one in progress. The nervous system can be worked with, not only observed.

Removing distraction mattered as much as adding practice. Quitting social media and passive scrolling created the internal quiet necessary for subtler responses to become visible. The signal was always there. The noise had been too loud to hear.

The experiment changed the experimenter. Running this study altered the researcher's relationship to their own automatic responses in ways that extend well beyond the documented data points. The map, once drawn, changes how you move through the territory.

Process Note — What the practice actually looked like

What I was actually doing, week by week, was a practice of interruption. Not meditation exactly, though that was part of it. More like: catching myself in the middle of an automatic response and asking one question. Is this happening now, or is this a stored response to something that already happened?

Most of the time, the answer was the second thing. The work of undoing conditioning isn't about stopping the response — it arrives faster than thought. The work is in what you do in the moment after. That gap — recognition — is the practice. Over time, the gap gets bigger. The plants didn't survive my inconsistency. This practice did, because it required no water, no schedule, no external conditions — just whatever was happening in front of me.

I set out to study whether emotional intention affects the growth of plants.

What I found is that it had already been affecting my growth for years.

Where does your body remember things that your mind has already moved on from?

Process Note — Why the question stays open

The plants died because I could not sustain the presence required by the experiment. Understanding why I could not sustain that presence became the experiment. And in following that question into rooms, staircases, windows, night roads, songs, old houses, and long histories, I found that the nervous system is an extraordinarily diligent record-keeper — and that it does not distinguish between records that are still relevant and records that have simply never been reviewed.

The work of this semester was reviewing them. Not erasing them. But bringing them into the light, naming their origin, and creating enough space between stimulus and response to make a different choice. That gap is where the actual experiment lives.

The original question remains open: does structured emotional intention, directed consistently toward a living system, produce measurable biological difference? I believe it does. I believe it because I have been running that experiment on myself, and the preliminary results are promising. The experiment is still running.
