I was going to study plants.

Three Dianthus. Three emotional climates. One carefully designed experiment about whether human intention shapes biological growth.

Process Note — Why this experiment?

I began this project to examine whether intention could function as a measurable variable. While reading The Secret Life of Plants, I was struck by Cleve Backster’s claim that a plant responded to the moment he formed intent, not to physical action. This suggested that emotional states might influence living systems in ways not typically measured.

To test this, I used three identical Dianthus plants under identical physical conditions, varying only my emotional interaction. One received positive attention, one negative emotional exposure, and one remained neutral.

I planned to track biological growth alongside daily emotional input, expecting any differences to appear in the plants.

The plants died by week seven.

But the experiment didn't.

Process Note — What actually happened

The first week went mostly as planned. Engaging with the affirmation plant felt strange at first but became easier. Avoiding the neutral plant felt even more unnatural. I did not meaningfully interact with the discharge plant, as I could not generate negative emotion on demand, which later proved important.

By the second week, the structure broke down under academic pressure. Care became inconsistent, logging stopped, and by week seven the plants had not survived.

In retrospect, the experiment shifted focus. Instead of measuring plant response, it revealed my inability to sustain consistent emotional presence, which became the key finding.

Trying to sustain emotional presence toward three potted plants, I found the same experiment already running on the self

The body had been storing emotional associations with places, sounds, and situations for years, retrieving them automatically and shaping behavior before conscious thought could catch up.

This echoed the Backster idea. His plant responded before any physical action, and similarly, my body reacted before I consciously processed the context. The mechanism is different, but the pattern is comparable: a system responding to stored experience the moment it senses something familiar.

The question then shifted to the self. What emotional data has the body stored, and can becoming aware of it change how it shapes present experience?

Process Note — The somatic memory framework

Somatic memory refers to emotional and physiological information stored in the nervous system through lived experience. Unlike declarative memory, it operates below conscious awareness, triggering responses faster than deliberate thought.

The issue arises when these stored patterns no longer match present conditions. A response shaped by past context continues to activate even when it is no longer relevant. The aim of this work was to identify such patterns, bring them into awareness, and observe whether recognition could reduce their intensity.

The Emotional Geography

Six locations. Each carrying a distinct somatic charge. The body's response is real. Its source, in most cases, is historical.

Hostel room — lights after midnight
Autopilot 7/10 — anxiety→After recognition 4/10

Lights left on past midnight trigger low-level dread and a sense of being trapped in wakefulness. This response comes from past sleep disruption caused by a previous roommate. Although the situation has changed, the body continues to react as if it has not.

Staircase window
Autopilot 6/10 — dread→After recognition 3/10

This space holds layered associations. It was once linked to calm, but a later interview introduced anticipatory dread. Recognizing these as separate layers made it possible to access the earlier sense of calm again.

Lift area window
Autopilot 7/10 — trapped/bored→After recognition 2/10

A strong feeling of confinement and boredom appeared immediately, before conscious thought. Through real-time reframing, the space shifted from restrictive to open. This was the fastest and most complete emotional reset observed.

Campus roads at night
Autopilot 6/10 — alertness→After recognition 5/10

Repeated monkey encounters have generalized a threat response. Even seeing someone carry food now triggers alertness. Because this is based on real repeated experience, it is more resistant to change.

Sister's house
Autopilot 6/10 — survival-anxiety→After recognition 4/10

This location was associated with scarcity due to past conditions of limited food and resources. Although things have improved, anticipatory anxiety still appears, showing that the body has not fully updated.

Parents' house and home state
Autopilot 8/10 — old feelings→After recognition 6/10

This location carries the densest emotional history. Familiar surroundings trigger past feelings before arrival. These responses are automatic and remain the most difficult to recondition.

Process Note — Data collection method

Triggers were recorded as they occurred rather than being intentionally sought. Each entry included the trigger, the intensity of the initial response on a scale of 1–10, its likely origin, and a second rating after conscious awareness was applied.

Supporting practices such as meditation and mindfulness were not used to generate data, but to enable the level of awareness required to observe these responses.

The Timeline

The data collected over the semester became less biological and more temporal, documenting how the experiment shifted over time rather than how the plants grew.

Early Semester

The project began with three Dianthus plants under different emotional conditions. The first week followed the intended structure, though it quickly became clear that negative emotional engagement could not be generated on command.

Weeks 2–3

Academic pressure and inconsistent routine disrupted the process. Watering became irregular, logging stopped, and the structure of the original experiment gradually collapsed.

Week 7

The plants died. By this point, it was already apparent that the project had shifted focus. The most important observation was not the plants' response, but my inability to sustain intentional emotional presence consistently.

Mid Semester

The methodology pivoted inward. Meditation, mindfulness, and deliberate self-observation became part of the process. Data collection continued through spontaneous emotional triggers tied to places, sounds, and situations.

Late Semester

Intentional auditory conditioning began. Certain songs became associated with calm and emotional regulation through repeated conscious reinforcement. The original logic of the plant experiment was now being applied internally.

End of Semester

Six locations and multiple songs were documented. A consistent pattern emerged: conscious recognition reduced the intensity of conditioned responses, but rarely removed them entirely. The deeper the conditioning, the more resistant it was to change.

Process Note — Reconstruction

This timeline was reconstructed retrospectively rather than documented in exact real time. The sequence is reliable even if specific dates are approximate. Rebuilding it afterwards revealed another important limitation: meaningful temporal analysis depends on consistent documentation.

The Data

Two categories of trigger documented across eleven weeks: spatial (places) and auditory (songs). Both produced automatic physiological responses. Both were measurable before and after conscious recognition was applied.

How the scale works

A 10 means the response still shapes behaviour, while a 1 means the emotional charge is minimal or no longer disruptive.

The second rating is never zero. Awareness does not erase the response, but it creates distance from it.

♪ The Auditory Data

Sound proved to be the strongest trigger in the study. Emotional responses to music appeared faster and more intense than responses tied to physical spaces.

Tracks from Banana Fish Original Soundtrack consistently triggered grief regardless of current mood, while The Adults Are Talking gradually lost its earlier emotional weight over time. This suggested that emotional associations can weaken or remap through changing circumstances.

Teahouse became an intentional conditioning case, repeatedly associated with calm until it began producing a reliable emotional response on its own.

The People Data

Relation/Bond triggers worked differently from spatial or auditory ones. They were activated through proximity, recall, or anticipation.

One relationship in particular contained the highest density of emotional history. Anger and irritation arrived before conscious evaluation, shaping reactions automatically. The deeper and longer the shared history, the more resistant the pattern became to change.

The intervention does not happen before the response, but immediately after it appears. The (my) response is observed rather than fully identified with. The emotional record remained, but the relationship and my reaction to it began to change.

Person B
This relationship had fewer accumulated emotional layers, making it easier to revise, but it was also formed in adulthood so there was more attachment. The response was tied to an event and not long-term conditioning, creating temporary anxiety and imbalance. Once I addressed the underlying issue, the emotional intensity reduced, making this one of the clearest revisions in the dataset.

Person C
This case differed from the others because there was no prior conditioning involved. The disruption came from a sudden conflict that was too immediate to process in real time. Unlike earlier entries, the emotional response reflected the present moment rather than stored memory. Over time the intensity reduced, but the experience was still actively being processed.

Process Note — Limitations of the Data

This dataset is entirely self-reported and based on subjective experience. There is no external validation or control group, and the intensity scale reflects perceived emotional response rather than measurable physiological change.

What the project offers is an account of what these experiences felt like from within. The limitations do not invalidate the findings, but define the scope within which they can be understood.

In Backster's framework,
I was the plant.

I began the experiment intending to study plants, but the uncontrolled variable was myself. My inconsistency, forgetfulness, and inability to sustain emotional presence disrupted the original design and revealed a more interesting question instead.

I realized I was constantly reacting to stimuli I had not consciously registered. The experiment became less about external systems and more about learning to ask: what am I actually responding to here?

Process Note — The experimental problem

There is no clear separation between the observer and the observed in this project. I was simultaneously the researcher, the subject, and the instrument through which the data was collected. Rather than treating this as a flaw, the experiment was built around it.

The places and relationships carrying the strongest emotional responses were usually those with the longest shared history. Over time, familiarity accumulates layered and often conflicting emotional associations. Because of this density, awareness-based revision becomes slower and more difficult, requiring the separation of present experience from stored memory.

What I Learned

Recognition reduces.
It does not erase.

Awareness reduced emotional intensity, but never erased it completely.

Older and deeper memories proved harder to recondition than newer ones.

The body consistently reacted before conscious thought.

Positive emotional associations could be intentionally built over time.

Reducing distraction made subtle emotional patterns easier to observe.

The experiment ultimately changed the researcher as much as the research itself.

Process Note — The Practice

The practice was less about meditation and more about interruption and focus. Whenever an automatic emotional response appeared, the question became: is this coming from the present moment, or from stored memory?

Most often, it was the latter. The goal was not to stop the response, but to recognize it after it appeared. Over time, that moment of recognition became easier and more immediate.

Unlike the original plant experiment, this practice did not depend on consistency of routine or external conditions. It only required attention to whatever was happening in the moment.

I set out to study whether emotional intention affects the growth of plants.

What I found is that it had already been affecting my growth for years.

Where does your body remember things that your mind has already moved on from?

Process Note — Why the question stays open

The original question remains open: does structured emotional intention, directed consistently toward a living system, produce measurable biological difference? I believe it does. I believe it because I have been running that experiment on myself, and the preliminary results are promising. The experiment is still running.
